Methods Library / Commodity-chain analysis

Clelland (2014):
Dark value in global commodity chains

A method for investigating how underpaid labour, unpaid reproductive work and uncosted ecological inputs contribute to the value embedded in globally produced commodities.

Toolkit status: Method documented · Reconstruction not yet undertaken

This profile describes Clelland's published approach and reports his estimates. The Toolkit has not independently reconstructed the calculations, recovered all underlying data or verified the results.

01 / The study

Donald A. Clelland's The Core of the Apple: Dark Value and Degrees of Monopoly in Global Commodity Chains examines the commodity chain of the first-generation Apple iPad, focusing on production during 2010–2011.

Clelland argues that conventional accounts of value added and profit capture overlook important inputs whose costs are borne by workers, households, communities and ecosystems rather than fully paid by firms.

He calls these underpaid and uncosted contributions dark value, distinguishing them from the monetarised flows of bright value visible in conventional accounts.

The study combines an analysis of the distribution of recorded revenue and costs across the iPad chain with estimates of hidden labour and ecological contributions.

02 / Research question

How do unequal relationships within a global commodity chain enable firms and consumers to benefit from labour and ecological inputs that are underpaid or excluded from conventional production costs?

03 / Unit of analysis and scope

Dimension Clelland's approach
Commodity First-generation Apple iPad
Period 2010–2011 production and associated source data
Unit of estimation US dollars per iPad
Production scope Detailed analysis concentrates on the first three supplier tiers, although the wider chain extends beyond them.
Geographical scope Global supplier relationships, with particular attention to production in China and other Asian economies.
Comparison Estimated production costs compared with counterfactual costs using US compensation or cost multipliers.

04 / How the method works

Step 1. Reconstruct the commodity chain

Clelland identifies the iPad's suppliers and production locations using product teardowns, Apple's supplier information, corporate materials and previous research. He distinguishes assembly, component manufacture and subcomponent production.

The reconstruction matters because the headquarters location of a supplier does not necessarily identify where production takes place.

Step 2. Account for visible value

The study estimates the distribution of recorded costs and gross profit margins across Apple, its suppliers and production workers.

Clelland also examines gross and operating profit margins as indicators of differential degrees of monopoly and monopsony within the chain.

Step 3. Estimate undercompensated labour

The study estimates the difference between actual labour costs and counterfactual costs calculated using US compensation benchmarks.

It distinguishes direct production workers from professional, managerial and other indirect production labour.

Step 4. Estimate additional externalities

Clelland extends the analysis beyond paid employment to estimate contributions associated with:

  • Unpaid household production and reproduction of labour.
  • Underpaid and unpaid informal-sector labour.
  • Unpaid ecological costs associated with production.

These estimates use additional assumptions about household work, wages, expenditure, pollution and comparative costs.

Step 5. Combine the estimates

The resulting estimates are assembled into a partial accounting of dark value embedded in each iPad. Clelland then considers how this value may benefit firms and final consumers.

Important distinction

The counterfactual estimates do not represent money observed moving between countries or firms. They estimate the value of underpaid and uncosted contributions using specified comparisons and assumptions.

05 / Published results

Clelland's Table 5 reports the following estimates of dark value per iPad.

Source of dark value Published estimate (US$ per iPad)
Waged labour $397
Professional, managerial and office labour $175
Labour subtotal $572
Household labour externalities $171
Underpaid and unpaid informal-sector labour $144
Unpaid ecological externalities $190
Externalities subtotal $505
Total estimated dark value $1,077

Source: Clelland (2014), Table 5, p. 103. Figures reproduce the author's reported estimates; they are not independent Toolkit calculations.

Clelland argues that the benefits of dark value are not confined to corporate profits. He proposes that a substantial portion reaches final consumers through prices lower than those that would prevail under his counterfactual production costs.

07 / Assumptions and limitations

Counterfactual benchmark

The labour estimates depend on the use of US compensation as a comparison. The choice of benchmark and the construction of multipliers materially affect the resulting estimates.

Incomplete supply-chain coverage

Clelland identifies a wider network of supplier tiers but concentrates detailed analysis on the first three. The published estimate is therefore a partial accounting rather than a complete measurement of every input in the chain.

Estimated rather than directly observed inputs

Several components rely on assumptions and approximations. For example, the ecological estimate applies an assumed pollution-cost proportion and a multiplier to approximate counterfactual costs.

Historical specificity

The figures concern a particular iPad model and production period. They should not be presented as measurements of Apple's current supply chain or of contemporary electronics production.

Interpretation of consumer benefits

Clelland's account of consumer surplus differs from the conventional definition based on willingness to pay. It refers to the benefit consumers receive from costs that are not fully reflected in commodity prices.

Interpretation caution

The $1,077 figure is an aggregate of estimates constructed using different inputs and assumptions. It should not be interpreted as an observed financial transfer, an independently established market price, or a directly measured profit figure.

08 / Relationship to other approaches

Clelland explicitly situates dark value as an extension of unequal-exchange theory, while challenging the assumption of internationally equal profit rates associated with Emmanuel's formulation.

The study also extends commodity-chain analysis beyond the distribution of recorded value added. Its focus is on surplus capture, unequal bargaining power and contributions excluded from conventional accounting.

For the Toolkit, this creates a useful point of comparison with methods that estimate unequal exchange through international wage differences, embodied resources or monetary trade flows.

These approaches do not necessarily measure the same object. Their estimates should not be combined without first specifying differences in units, boundaries, counterfactuals and possible overlaps.

09 / Reconstruction and verification

The following tasks remain outstanding. Listing them does not imply that they have already been completed.

  • Recover the underlying sources used for the published tables.
  • Reconstruct the calculations in Tables 1–5 and Appendix Tables 6–7.
  • Document all multipliers and intermediate assumptions.
  • Check rounding and internal arithmetic consistency.
  • Examine sensitivity to alternative wage benchmarks and ecological assumptions.
  • Assess possible overlap between categories of dark value.
  • Distinguish successful numerical reproduction from independent verification of source data.
Current verification statement

The paper has been identified and its method documented. No computational reconstruction or independent verification is claimed by this profile.
