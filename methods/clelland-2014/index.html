Methods Library / Commodity-chain analysis

Clelland (2014):
Dark value in global commodity chains

A documented method for analysing visible surplus capture and hidden labour, household and ecological contributions within a global commodity chain.

Toolkit status: Method documented · Reconstruction not yet undertaken

This profile describes Clelland's published approach and reports his estimates. The Toolkit has checked the article’s descriptions and published summary figures, but has not independently reconstructed the underlying calculations, recovered all source data or verified the results.

01 / The study

Donald A. Clelland’s The Core of the Apple: Degrees of Monopoly and Dark Value in Global Commodity Chains (2014) examines the commodity chain of the first-generation Apple iPad.

Clelland distinguishes bright value—monetarised costs, prices and surplus recorded within commodity chains—from dark value: hidden contributions associated with underpaid labour, labour reproduction and ecological externalities.

His argument concerns not only the existence of dark value but how it can be transformed into bright value and how its benefits are distributed. He uses degrees of monopoly (control over price markups) and degrees of monopsony (control over input-cost markdowns) to analyse unequal surplus capture among firms. He also argues that some benefits reach final consumers through lower prices.

The case combines an analysis of visible costs and corporate margins with estimates of undercompensated labour, unpaid reproductive contributions and ecological externalities.

02 / Research question

Toolkit formulation of Clelland’s inquiry: How are bright and dark value extracted and distributed within the iPad commodity chain, and how do differential degrees of monopoly and monopsony shape the benefits captured by firms and consumers?

04 / How the method works

Toolkit reconstruction

These five steps organise Clelland’s procedure for readers; they are not a numbered sequence reproduced from the article.

Step 1. Reconstruct the commodity chain

Identify suppliers, production sites and relationships using product teardowns, Apple’s supplier information, corporate websites and previous iProduct research. Distinguish company headquarters from actual production locations, and extend coverage to indirect labour and the third supplier tier.

Step 2. Analyse bright value and surplus capture

Estimate recorded production costs, prices and gross and operating margins across Apple and its suppliers. Clelland uses these margins as indicators in an argument about differential degrees of monopoly; they are not direct measurements of monopsony power or net transfers.

Step 3. Estimate dark value from undercompensated labour

Estimate direct production wages and indirect professional and managerial compensation across the first three supplier tiers. Apply US compensation benchmarks to obtain counterfactual costs; subtract estimated actual labour costs to derive the labour dark-value estimates in Table 3.

Step 4. Estimate other externalised contributions

Model unpaid household production and reproduction of labour, informal-sector services and ecological externalities. These estimates use additional assumptions about unpaid work, minimum wages, expenditure, pollution and comparative costs. Their accounting convention differs from the labour calculation (see Section 7).

Step 5. Summarise and interpret the estimates

Combine the categories in a partial accounting of dark value per iPad (Table 5), then examine Clelland’s argument that benefits can accrue to firms and be passed to final consumers through lower prices.

Important distinction

These are model-based counterfactual estimates, not observed payments moving between countries or firms. They should not be interpreted as cash profits or as a directly measured alternative retail price.

05 / Published results

Clelland reports the following estimates in Table 5 (p. 103). The Toolkit has checked their transcription and the arithmetic of the published subtotals; it has not independently reproduced the underlying calculations.

Source of dark valuePublished estimate (US$ per iPad)
Waged labour$397
Professional, managerial and headquarters office labour$175
Labour subtotal$572
Household labour externalities$171
Underpaid and unpaid informal-sector labour$144
Unpaid ecological externalities$190
Externalities subtotal$505
Total estimated dark value$1,077

The published arithmetic is consistent: $397 + $175 = $572; $171 + $144 + $190 = $505; and $572 + $505 = $1,077.

Two calculation conventions: Table 3 derives labour dark value by subtracting estimated actual costs ($80) from US counterfactual costs ($652), yielding $572. Table 4 instead labels multiplied counterfactual values for household, informal and ecological inputs as dark value, without displaying the same subtraction. The implications remain an open reconstruction question.

Clelland argues that a substantial share of the benefits associated with dark value reaches final consumers through lower prices. This is the author’s interpretation, not an independently verified allocation by the Toolkit.

06 / Data and calculation inputs

The study draws on several source types and modelling assumptions rather than one downloadable dataset. Details appear in the table notes and Appendix Tables 6–7.

ComponentInputs and construction
Chain and visible valueProduct teardowns, Apple supplier information, corporate reports and earlier iProduct studies; component prices and corporate gross and operating margins (Tables 1–2).
Direct labourEstimated labour costs in Tiers 1–3, wage and benefit comparisons, and US compensation multipliers (Table 3; Appendix Tables 6–7).
Indirect labourEstimates of professional and managerial compensation embedded in gross margins, including an approximately 40% salary-cost proxy and assumptions about local staff.
Household labourAssumptions about child-rearing, unpaid household work, minimum wages and reproduction of labour; $7 and $12 inputs multiplied by nine to give $171 (Table 4).
Informal-sector labourAn estimated $48 paid to Chinese workers per iPad; a 33% respending assumption produces about $16, multiplied by nine to give $144 (Table 4).
Ecological externalitiesA World Bank estimate of pollution costs informs an assumed 9% rate applied to $207 of Chinese production costs (approximately $19); a tenfold cleanup-cost multiplier gives $190 (Table 4).

These are Clelland’s reported inputs and assumptions, not independently validated Toolkit measurements. Recovering the underlying sources and intermediate calculations is part of a future reconstruction.

07 / Assumptions and limitations

US counterfactual

Labour estimates depend on selected US wages, benefits, occupational comparisons and multipliers. The resulting gaps are conditional estimates, not directly observed monetary transfers.

Incomplete and estimated chain data

Some production sites and costs are inferred. Detailed coverage is limited to the first three supplier tiers, leaving upstream and ancillary contributions outside the quantitative accounting.

Indirect labour and social reproduction

Professional and managerial estimates depend partly on assumptions about salary shares within gross margins and the location of employees. Household and informal-sector figures depend on assumptions about unpaid work, minimum wages and spending patterns.

Ecological approximation

Clelland calls the ecological calculation a rough measure: no direct monetary study of the relevant costs was available. The $190 estimate depends on the assumed pollution share and US cleanup-cost multiplier (pp. 102–103).

Accounting convention and possible overlap

Table 3 subtracts actual labour costs from counterfactual costs, whereas Table 4 reports multiplied counterfactual amounts as dark value without displaying a comparable subtraction. The Toolkit has not established whether this is a conceptual choice, an accounting inconsistency or what effect an alternative convention would have. Potential overlap between categories also remains to be investigated; double counting has not been demonstrated.

Historical and interpretive limits

The estimates concern a specific early iPad model and historical production network, not contemporary Apple production. Clelland’s use of consumer surplus refers to hidden value benefiting buyers through lower prices, rather than the conventional willingness-to-pay definition (p. 104, note 37).

How to read $1,077

This is Clelland’s aggregate model estimate, not an observed financial transfer, additional Apple profit or independently established market price. Its component calculations have not been independently reconstructed.

08 / Relationship to other approaches

Clelland presents dark value as an extension of unequal-exchange theory and explicitly challenges Emmanuel’s assumption of internationally equal profit rates (p. 83, note 3). His commodity-chain approach examines unequal bargaining power and surplus capture alongside underpaid and externalised contributions.

Toolkit comparison: This commodity-specific method can be compared with approaches using international wage differences, embodied resource flows or monetary trade flows. They do not necessarily estimate the same object. Comparison requires attention to units, production and geographical boundaries, counterfactuals and possible category overlap. These comparisons are the Toolkit’s framing, not a claim that Clelland evaluated the other methods in this library.

09 / Reconstruction and verification

Current status: Published method documented; independent reconstruction not completed

The Toolkit has checked this profile’s conceptual description, published figures and stated assumptions against the article. Table 5 values and its subtotals have been checked for transcription and arithmetic. This does not verify the source data, reproduce the underlying model or establish that its assumptions are empirically justified.

A future reconstruction would need to recover original sources, reproduce Tables 1–5 and Appendix Tables 6–7, document intermediate calculations and rounding, investigate the differing conventions in Tables 3 and 4, test sensitivity to alternative assumptions, and assess possible overlap among categories.

Successful reproduction of published numbers would still be distinct from independent verification of source data and interpretation.
